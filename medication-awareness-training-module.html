Lesson 1 of 5

Prompt, never administer — your role and your protection

Objective: understand the single rule at the heart of the Medication Prompting Policy (CARE-20) — Liberty Living prompts, we never administer — and why it protects the Service User and protects you.

One rule, no exceptions

Everything in this module flows from one sentence: the Service User must remove and take their own medication themselves. Your job is to prompt — to remind, to encourage, to make it easy. Their job is everything else: opening the pack, taking out the tablets, putting them in their own mouth. That line is non-negotiable, and it never moves — not for a kind reason, not for a busy day, not because a family member asked.

In plain English You bring the reminder, the unopened blister pack, and a glass of water. The Service User does everything else — their hands, their decision, every time.

Why the line exists

You are a Health Care Assistant, not a nurse. You are not trained, insured, or legally permitted to administer medication — and administering includes things that feel small and helpful, like popping a tablet out of its blister or placing it in someone's hand. A missed dose or a double dose can cause serious harm or death. The rule is strict precisely because the consequences are severe.

The rule protects you too

While you stay inside the policy, you are fully protected. If something goes wrong with a Service User's medication and all you did was prompt, record, and call the office when unsure — you did your job perfectly. Step outside the policy, even with the best of intentions, and you are on your own: the Service User is at risk, and so are you.

Lesson 2 of 5

The green list and the red lines

Objective: know exactly what you are permitted to do during a medication prompt — and the actions that are never permitted, no matter who asks.

CARE-20 draws the line in bright ink. Learn both sides of it until they're automatic.

You ARE permitted to

  • Remind the Service User it is time to take their medication.
  • Ask if they have already taken their medication.
  • Bring the medication to them in its original, unopened blisters.
  • Provide a drink of water to take with their medication.
  • Follow the instructions in their Care Plan.
  • Record that you provided a prompt in the CRM system.

You are NEVER permitted to

  • Open or remove medication from blister packs, bottles, or any packaging.
  • Place medication into the Service User's hand or mouth.
  • Prepare, sort, or organise medication in any way.
  • Crush medication — under any circumstances.
  • Make any decision about dosage, timing, or whether medication should be taken.
Crushing: zero exceptions Crushing medication is strictly prohibited regardless of the reason. Not if the Service User struggles to swallow. Not if the family says "we always do it this way." Not if it's mixed into food or a drink. Crushing changes how a medicine is absorbed and can be dangerous or fatal. If swallowing is a problem, that is clinical information the office needs — call, don't crush.

Timing and dosage are never your call

You never decide whether a dose should be taken, skipped, delayed, or doubled. If the Care Plan timing doesn't fit the visit, if the Service User says "I'll take it later," if anything about dose or timing is unclear — that decision belongs to a clinician. Record what happened, and call the office.

Lesson 3 of 5

Medications you must never be involved with

Objective: recognise the medications and treatments that are completely off-limits — you must never administer, prepare, or assist with them in any way.

Some medications are so high-risk that even a well-meaning "small help" can cause serious harm. For everything below, your involvement is zero — no handling, no preparing, no assisting. Tap each card to see why.

Insulin & blood sugar +

Injections and glucose monitoring. Insulin dosing depends on blood sugar readings — get either wrong and the Service User can collapse into a medical emergency. Only a trained clinician or the Service User themselves may do this. If they cannot manage it, call the office.

Warfarin & titrated doses +

Blood thinners and any medication where the dose changes based on test results. The "right" dose changes week to week. A dose that was correct last Tuesday can cause internal bleeding today. Never involved — ever.

Patches & eye drops +

Medicated patches and all eye drops. Applying either one counts as administering medication. It does not matter how simple it looks or how often they ask — this is clinical work, not prompting.

Suppositories & enemas +

Any internally administered medication. Completely outside the HCA role. If a Service User needs help with these, that is a clinical need — report it to the office so the right professional is arranged.

Soluble medicines +

Anything mixed into water or food — like Movicol. Mixing a medicine is preparing a medicine, even when it "looks like making a drink." The family may see it as harmless; the policy does not. Never mix, never stir, never hand it over.

Oxygen, inhalers & nebulisers +

All breathing treatments and equipment. Setting flow rates, loading nebuliser doses, or helping with inhalers are clinical acts. If breathing equipment seems wrong or the Service User is struggling — that is an emergency conversation with the office (or 999), not a DIY fix.

Prescribed creams & ointments +

Steroid creams and any prescribed topical treatment. If it was prescribed, applying it is administering it. Ordinary moisturising as part of personal care is different — if you are not sure which one you are looking at, stop and call the office.
The test is simple If it goes into, onto, or through the Service User's body and a doctor prescribed it — it is not yours to touch.

Lesson 4 of 5

When something isn't right with the blister pack

Objective: follow the correct steps — stop, don't guess, call — when a blister pack day is missing, already gone, or incorrect, or when a Service User can no longer manage their own pack.

Imagine this

"It's Thursday morning. You bring Eileen her blister pack with breakfast — but Thursday's compartment is already empty. Eileen looks at it, puzzled: 'I don't remember taking those.' Friday's tablets are sitting right there."

Every instinct says fix it — give her Friday's tablets, she needs her medication. That instinct is exactly what CARE-20 exists to stop. If Eileen already took Thursday's dose an hour ago, "borrowing" from Friday means a double dose — and you have no way of knowing which situation you're in.

The pathway — every time

1
StopDo not prompt the Service User to take medication from any other day. Do not guess. Do not try to resolve it yourself.
2
Call the office immediately — 01-416 3717Before doing anything further. The office will tell you exactly what happens next.
3
Follow the instructions you're givenThe decision about what the Service User takes belongs to a clinician — never to you, and never to the family.
4
Record and reportDocument what you found and what you did in the CRM, and report it to your Supervisor without delay.

"They just can't manage the pack anymore"

Sometimes there's nothing wrong with the pack — the Service User's hands, sight, or memory can no longer manage it. Arthritis that can't pop a blister. Confusion about which day it is. It feels natural to step in and help. Do not step in. Do not try to help. Call your Supervisor or the Manager immediately. A Service User who can no longer self-manage medication needs a clinical reassessment — that is an escalation, not a judgement call you make on the spot.

Remember Anything that looks wrong with a blister pack — missing day, extra tablets, wrong name on the label, damaged packaging — is a stop-and-call moment. You will never be criticised for calling. You will always be supported for stopping.

Lesson 5 of 5

Pressure, refusals and the paper trail

Objective: respond correctly when someone pressures you to break the rules, when a Service User refuses medication — and record everything that matters.

Imagine this

"Tom's daughter meets you at the door. 'Dad's been spitting out his tablets. Just crush them into his porridge, will you? The last carer always did it — he never even notices.' She's stressed, she loves her father, and she's asking you to do something that feels kind."

This is the hardest moment in medication work: a real person, asking nicely, for something absolutely prohibited.

What you do

  • Stay warm and calm — she isn't the enemy.
  • Explain kindly: "I'm not allowed to do that — it could genuinely harm him. Let me get the office involved so we can sort this properly."
  • Call the office immediately and tell them exactly what was asked.
  • Record the conversation in the CRM and report it to your Supervisor.

What you never do

  • Comply — not with crushing, opening packaging, or handling medication in any way.
  • Agree "just this once" to keep the peace.
  • Assume it's fine because "the last carer did it."
  • Keep the request to yourself.

Pressure from a Service User or family member never overrides this policy. When you say no and call the office, you will be fully supported — every time. And there's a bigger picture: a Service User regularly spitting out tablets or unable to swallow needs a medication review. Your phone call is what makes that happen.

Refusals and concerns

A Service User is always entitled to say no to their medication — you never persuade beyond a gentle prompt, and you never force. But a refusal is never "nothing": any concerns, missed doses, or refusals must be documented in the CRM and reported to your Supervisor without delay. The record you write is what lets the clinical team spot a pattern before it becomes a crisis.
